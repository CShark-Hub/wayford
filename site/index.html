It works perfectly. For Ada.

Every app works for the people who built it. Wayford sends an AI agent through your web app toward one goal, under on what it can read, tap and take in, enforced in code. It shows who gets across, and the assumption behind every no.

How it works

Readable isn't reachable.

Scanners check rules. Test bots check flows. Neither tells you who it works for.

Most teams test as Ada: every limit off, every pattern known, never lost in a menu she designed. People who get stuck rarely tell you. In one UK survey of disabled internet users, most said they simply leave.

          We claim

          • a measurement;
          • hypotheses, each linked to its evidence;
          • the same perception decisions for the same ;
          • a source on every limit, or the words "our assumption".

          We don't claim

          • how real people behave;
          • a replacement for user research;
          • compliance;
          • that the model's answers replay.

          A model given less input still reasons well. A person leans in, zooms, asks someone. The model doesn't, unless a rule says so.

          Surveys, standards, studies and law

          1. Of disabled internet users with access needs in the UK, 69% say they click away from a site with access barriers, and 8% say they contacted the site owner about them. Participants chose to take part in an online survey, and the figures are what they reported.

            Williams and Brownlow, The Click-Away Pound Report 2019, Table 1 (Freeney Williams, February 2020) · press release

          2. Vera's 3:1 is SC 1.4.3's level for large text; Zoya's 7:1 is SC 1.4.6's level for normal text.

            WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) and SC 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced); the standard itself: W3C WCAG 2.2

          3. Target sizes: WCAG's 24 px minimum and 44 px enhanced level, with Vera's 32 px, our assumption, between them.

            WCAG 2.2, SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) and SC 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced)

          4. Vera's 16 px and Zoya's 24 px text limits are our own numbers, set against how print size relates to sight. The paper gives no pixel values.

            Legge & Bigelow, "Does print size matter for reading?", Journal of Vision 11(5):8, 2011: free full text · doi:10.1167/11.5.8

          5. Milo's 44 px target limit: 44 × 44 pt, the default control size on iPhone and iPad.

            Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Accessibility (Mobility)

          6. Zoya's 48 px target limit: touch targets of at least 48 × 48 dp.

            Material Design 3, Accessibility designing: structure

          7. Kai's memory of 2 steps is our assumption, set against a working memory of about four items.

            Cowan, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 2001: doi:10.1017/S0140525X01003922

          8. Automated testing often cannot assess whether people with cognitive or learning disabilities can use the content.

            W3C, Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (Working Group Note, 29 Apr 2021), usability testing

          9. The European Accessibility Act applies from 28 June 2025, to e-commerce and consumer banking among others.

            Directive (EU) 2019/882, Article 2(2)(d) and (f), Article 31(2)

          10. axe, the accessibility scanner named in "Why not ask ChatGPT".

            axe-core by Deque

          11. Wayford, the village on the River Axe in Somerset.

            Wikipedia, Wayford
